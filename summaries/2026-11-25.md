### When AI Writes the Code, What Counts as Evidence? - (Stack Overflow Blog)

https://stackoverflow.blog/2026/11/25/when-ai-writes-the-code-what-counts-as-evidence/

# Summary

As AI coding agents increasingly solve problems independently, there's a risk that developers gain working code but lose the valuable institutional knowledge that comes from understanding *why* solutions work. When agents silently fix errors during private sessions, the investigation and learning disappear—even if the patch succeeds. The article argues that software teams need to capture and preserve evidence from these AI-generated solutions: documented failures, the reasoning behind fixes, and verified explanations that future developers can reference.

The author proposes a system where coding agents' troubleshooting sessions are recorded with proper context, searched against existing knowledge before creating new questions, and reviewed by humans before being stored as reusable knowledge. Success shouldn't be measured by counting generated answers or questions, but by whether recurring problems are actually solved more efficiently because the team retained what it learned. Human editorial judgment remains essential—not every successful command reveals the true root cause, and technical knowledge must be verified and contextualized to remain useful beyond the immediate incident.

### Idempotency when you don't control the other end - (Stack Overflow Blog)

https://stackoverflow.blog/2026/11/24/idempotency-when-you-don-t-control-the-other-end/

# Summary

This article explores the challenge of ensuring idempotency (safe retries) when calling third-party APIs that don't support idempotency keys—a common problem for maesn, a company that acts as an intermediary between customers and 30+ accounting/ERP systems. While idempotency is straightforward when you control both sides of an API (like Stripe), the real difficulty emerges when calling external systems during timeouts: the caller cannot determine whether a request succeeded or failed, making it impossible to safely retry without risking duplicate entries in critical systems like accounting ledgers.

The article details three common workarounds—lookup by document number, checking before writing, and cleanup afterwards—and explains why each fails in practice. Document numbers aren't truly unique across suppliers, pre-write checks create timing race conditions with slow systems, and post-hoc cleanup is unreliable and risky during month-end closures. Ultimately, only the accounting system itself can solve this problem by implementing idempotency keys, since intermediaries can only push the uncertainty downstream. The author argues providers neglect idempotency not from carelessness but because traditional UI-based workflows rarely encounter duplicate submissions, unlike automated integrations that retry at scale.

### Prompt Injection Fixes Need Regression Tests - (Stack Overflow Blog)

https://stackoverflow.blog/2026/11/24/prompt-injection-fixes-need-regression-tests/

# Summary

This article argues that prompt injection vulnerabilities in AI systems require regression testing because fixes can unexpectedly resurface when models upgrade, prompts change, or data connectors shift—even without code modifications. Unlike traditional security reviews that occur annually, agentic systems evolve continuously, necessitating automated test suites that run on every build to catch regressions before release. The article emphasizes treating model version upgrades as release events and implementing zero-trust validation for inter-agent communication.

The key practical recommendation is for teams to convert every previously patched injection attack into a test fixture that runs automatically whenever any part of the system changes. This approach requires splitting ownership: security teams define what constitutes safe behavior, while delivery teams own the gate by running regression tests before deployment. Coverage must include multiple attack vectors—user-supplied prompts, infected external data, and persistent threats in long-term memory—to prevent vulnerabilities from slipping through production.

### A Reboot Is Still a Deployment - (Stack Overflow Blog)

https://stackoverflow.blog/2026/11/24/a-reboot-is-still-a-deployment/

# Summary

This article argues that rebooting a server should be treated as a deployment, not just routine maintenance, and should be verified with actual application work rather than just checking if the service starts. The author contends that reboots change more than just code—they can affect kernels, volumes, and dependencies—so teams should require a completed job (like processing a queue item) as proof of successful recovery, not just a running process check.

The article uses a practical example of a queue-backed export service with two workers to illustrate what can go wrong during maintenance windows. It demonstrates how a simple health check (process is running) can mask deeper problems (unavailable volumes, failed dependencies) and shows why teams need explicit plans for handling incomplete jobs, verifying dependencies with systemd, and testing that workers can actually complete work after a restart. The author emphasizes that rolling back to a previous kernel won't undo completed jobs already written to durable storage, potentially creating duplicate work if recovery procedures aren't carefully coordinated.

### Windows Loudness Equalization, reverse-engineered and ported to PipeWire - (Hacker News)

https://github.com/xoxorwr/pw-loudness-eq

# Summary

This GitHub repository contains a reverse-engineered port of Windows' Loudness Equalization feature to PipeWire (a Linux audio system). The project aims to even out volume levels across different applications and audio sources, so quiet and loud parts play at similar levels. It consists of three main components: a PipeWire loudness module that processes individual app playback streams, an output limiter to prevent clipping from the volume boost, and an optional GTK3 GUI for configuration.

The implementation uses a sophisticated broadband automatic gain control algorithm that analyzes audio in frequency bands and applies targeted gain adjustments, complemented by a look-ahead limiter ported from Windows' AudioEngine.dll. Installation requires PipeWire 1.4+, GTK3, and standard development tools, with settings managed through PipeWire metadata that persist across restarts and can be controlled via command-line or the graphical interface.

### Predicting LoRA size for any dataset - (Hacker News)

https://engram.com/blog/lora-size

# Summary

This article presents a theoretical and practical framework for predicting the LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) rank required to fine-tune language models on any given dataset. The researchers develop a predictive theory grounded in information theory, showing that LoRA capacity depends on how many bits are needed to encode a dataset under a base model. Using Shannon's source coding theorem, they demonstrate that a rank-r LoRA adapter has approximately linear capacity growth at low ranks, with diminishing returns at higher ranks—unlike full-parameter fine-tuning which scales linearly with parameter count.

To validate their theory on complex transformer architectures, the authors employ an innovative approach using arithmetic coding to generate synthetic datasets with exactly N bits of information under a base model. By training LoRA adapters on these precisely-sized datasets, they empirically measure capacity and demonstrate their predictions hold on real datasets. This work enables practitioners to determine the minimum LoRA rank needed for any dataset, providing significant practical advantages including reduced storage, faster inference, and more efficient model customization at scale.

### I checked Clojure's core with Lean and found unreported bugs - (Hacker News)

https://s.conao3.com/blog/2026/e4f733

# Summary

A developer applied formal verification techniques using Lean 4 and differential testing to Clojure's core library and discovered 17 previously unreported bugs, despite Clojure's persistent data structures being widely used since version 1.0. The bugs were found by modeling Clojure's Java code in Lean to search for counterexamples in arithmetic operations, and by performing differential testing against standard Java collections. Notably, the bugs did not exist in the core tree data structures themselves (HAMT and vectors), but rather in peripheral code and at the intersection of different mechanisms, such as hashing and equality checking.

Key bugs identified include: an infinite loop in `reduce` on `vector-of` caused by a bit mask that incorrectly clears bits beyond bit 16, a HAMT collision node that records the wrong hash when dissociating keys with identical hashes, inconsistent behavior in `removeIf` depending on whether type hints are provided, and integer overflow issues in drop operations. The author provided Lean proofs for the fixed versions and reproduced all 17 bugs on Clojure version 1.12.7, demonstrating that formal verification can uncover subtle bugs even in mature, heavily-used codebases.

### 1Password Security Incident - (Hacker News)

https://1password.com/blog/update-on-nov-2026-security-incident

# 1Password Security Incident Summary

On November 19, 2026, 1Password discovered that an attacker gained access to an employee's GitHub account and used it to push malicious code to backend services, allowing them to download limited customer account information. The compromised data included customer names, email addresses, account identifiers, and non-secret account metadata, but crucially, **customer vault contents, encryption keys, and passwords were not affected** and remain fully secure.

In response, 1Password disabled the compromised account, rotated all internal credentials, enhanced security controls, and increased the length of account recovery codes from 6 to 10 alphanumeric characters to prevent brute-force attacks. The company emphasizes that users do not need to take any action and recommends remaining vigilant against phishing emails. No evidence of actual account takeovers was found, and the company reaffirms that its zero-knowledge encryption architecture ensures that even a backend breach cannot expose customer vault secrets.

https://jayd.ml/2026/11/14/rockbox-on-yp3.html

# Summary

The author successfully ported Rockbox, a custom firmware for MP3 players, to a cheap $9 undocumented Chinese MP3 player (YP3) using Claude AI with minimal personal involvement. The device has severe hardware constraints—only 512 KB of SRAM, a mysterious dual-core CPU, and 2 MB of flash—making it smaller than any existing Rockbox target. Through a software-only reverse engineering approach using Ghidra and AI agents, they achieved a working port that plays audio and supports most common codecs, though with compromises like no background music/plugins concurrency and reduced features.

The project demonstrates the potential of LLMs for embedded systems work: Claude reverse-engineered the Bluetooth stack overnight, optimized hot code loops for the slow flash storage, and subsequently ported Rockbox to another player (TS47) in just 4 hours using a reusable toolkit. The author's role was primarily project management and acting as a "meat robot" to test, reboot, and provide feedback, while the AI handled the complex firmware development and driver writing.
