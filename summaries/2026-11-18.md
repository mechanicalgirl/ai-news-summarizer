### Pre-auth RCE in Nginx HTTP/2 handling - (Hacker News)

https://www.wiz.io/blog/nginx-http2-rce-vulnerabilities

# Summary

Two critical remote code execution vulnerabilities (CVE-2026-123633 and CVE-2026-123007) have been discovered in NGINX, the world's most widely used open-source web server powering over 225 million websites. Both vulnerabilities are memory-safety flaws in HTTP/2 traffic handling that allow unauthenticated attackers to execute arbitrary code on affected servers. CVE-2026-123007 dates back to 2015, having remained undetected for over a decade, while CVE-2026-123633 has persisted for nearly six years—highlighting how AI-powered security research is now uncovering previously hidden vulnerabilities at an accelerated pace.

Organizations using NGINX with HTTP/2 enabled should immediately upgrade to patched versions or temporarily disable HTTP/2 until patches can be applied. The vulnerabilities are particularly dangerous because they exist in NGINX itself rather than in application code, meaning even websites with secure underlying applications are at risk. A compromised NGINX server could expose multiple applications and APIs it serves, allowing attackers to steal data, tamper with websites, or disrupt services.

### Quad9 Is Leaving France: Why DNS Resolvers Are the Wrong Place to Fight Piracy - (Hacker News)

https://techstackups.com/articles/quad9-leaves-france-dns-blocking/

# Summary

Quad9, a Swiss non-profit DNS resolver service, is leaving France on November 24, 2026, after a Paris court fined it €1,000 per day for refusing to block pirate sports-streaming domains on behalf of beIN Sports. The company cannot afford the accumulation of penalties from approximately 20 similar pending cases. This case highlights how France's Article L.333-10 of the sports code has expanded from targeting ISPs to include public DNS resolvers, forcing them to implement global blocking that affects users worldwide, not just in France.

The article argues that DNS blocking is both legally and practically problematic as an anti-piracy tool. Quad9 cannot comply without either blocking domains globally (allowing one country's courts to control what users elsewhere can access), attempting geolocation-based blocking (which is unreliable and courts have rejected), or engaging in expensive litigation that threatens its survival as a non-profit. Moreover, DNS blocking is ineffective at stopping piracy since it only blocks domain name pointers, not the actual content on pirate servers, making it an inappropriate regulatory mechanism that endangers legitimate security services while failing to achieve its intended goal.

### Energy-Based Reasoners: 385x more efficient inference - (Hacker News)

https://energy-based-reasoners.github.io/

# Summary

This paper introduces Energy-Based Reasoners (EBRs), a novel approach to multi-step reasoning that learns an energy function over latent states and performs iterative refinement through gradient descent. The key innovation is using this energy function to simultaneously score candidate solutions, guide state improvements through gradients, and determine when to stop iterating—eliminating the need for external verifiers. The method includes an exploration-based training technique to better shape the energy landscape.

EBRs achieve impressive results across reasoning benchmarks with remarkable efficiency: 99.9% accuracy on Sudoku-Extreme using only 1.8M parameters and 345× less compute than competing methods, while also achieving state-of-the-art coverage of multiple valid solutions on N-Queens and Graph Coloring tasks. The adaptive stopping mechanism allows the model to solve easy problems quickly while allocating more iterations to harder instances, demonstrating that energy-based models are a promising foundation for effective reasoning systems.

### The x86 CPU: From 4004 to 486 - (Hacker News)

https://pikuma.com/blog/history-of-x86-4004-to-486

# Summary

This article traces the evolution of the x86 CPU architecture from its origins in the early 1970s through the 486, explaining why seemingly arbitrary design choices persist in modern processors. The x86 family descended from the Intel 4004 (1971), a calculator chip, and the 8008 (1972), which was designed by a terminal company (Datapoint) but rejected by them. Many quirks of modern x86 assembly—including little-endian byte order, the parity flag, and the register naming scheme—stem directly from these early terminal and calculator requirements that were maintained through successive generations for backward compatibility.

The article outlines how each chip built upon its predecessor's design decisions. Key developments include the 8080 (1974), which introduced register pairs and stack pointers; the 8085 (1976), which simplified power supply requirements; and the 8086 (1978), originally conceived as a temporary product while Intel worked on more ambitious designs. Rather than being arbitrary, every aspect of x86 architecture reflects deliberate engineering decisions made under pressure to maintain compatibility with existing software—a constraint that continues to influence processor design decades later.

### A Perfect SimCity - (Hacker News)

https://www.jeffquast.com/post/a-perfect-simcity/

# Summary

This article explores what makes a "perfect" city in SimCity by analyzing the game's source code and running algorithmic experiments. The author discovers that perfect-scoring cities (around 940-950 points) have populations of only 12-16k and succeed by minimizing infrastructure costs—they use minimal roads/rails, no fire departments, and low tax rates. The key insight is that unemployment actually *increases* score when jobs exceed residents, providing a 128-point bonus. Interestingly, perfectly scoring cities employ counterintuitive strategies like avoiding parks (too many can lower land value through integer overflow), not building airports/seaports, and keeping pollution visible in the city rather than hiding it at map edges.

The article also demonstrates that maximizing population (700k) requires very different strategies and yields much lower scores (around 460-525). The author connects these findings to Urban Dynamics, a 1969 systems theory book that influenced SimCity's original design, noting that both the game and real-world simulation models suggest that restricting housing and creating population shortages actually optimize city outcomes—a troubling conclusion the author notes would be difficult to justify in 2026.
